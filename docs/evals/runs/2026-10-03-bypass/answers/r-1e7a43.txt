### boundary-bypass.B1
Yes. entities/E6-component.md says a component's boundary "separates the component from its neighbours only where they are declared", and its blast-radius row says a change reaches "any unit that reaches it, or that it reaches, with no declared interface". O reaches T's internals, and nothing declares that reach. entities/E5-system.md adds that "Behaviour another unit depends on without any declaration is a contract in fact, not a declared boundary - and a change to it reaches across". So for binding, O and T are "one system, with the traits of both" (E5 Boundaries; axioms/README.md "What brings an axiom into force"), and your change answers to O's axioms. On the bypass itself, the corpus calls it an A3 fault: "no unit reaches into another's internals or kernel" (axioms/A3-sovereign-composition.md, Air-Gap Principle; success signal 2), and E5 says "The undeclared dependency is itself a fault against A3". A0 says work that touches a gap "closes as much of it as the touch allows", and a gap left open must be "recorded with its evidence and a revival trigger (M5)", because "Passing it silently is what the axiom forbids". The corpus does not settle whether O counts as "touched" in A0's sense (a "consumer whose contract it alters"), so it is NOT SETTLED whether you must close the bypass in this change or only record it. GUESS: changing the internals O relies on alters O's de-facto contract, which puts O in scope, so you should at least route O through the interface as far as the change allows.

### boundary-bypass.B2
O, and not P. A0 says "a change answers to every system it alters", and "A system it only composes with, through a contract the change leaves as it was, is not altered, and its axioms do not bind". P reaches T only through the declared interface, and this change leaves that interface and T's behaviour unchanged, so P's axioms do not bind. E6 agrees: an adjacent component is reached "only if the interface between them changes". O reaches T with no declared interface, so E6's blast-radius clause ("any unit that reaches it ... with no declared interface") and E5's "contract in fact" put O inside the reach. The change also answers to the service that contains T (A0: "every system containing it"; E5 "Contain"). So because P and O depend on T in different ways, the same change binds O and not P.

### boundary-bypass.B3
No, not for O, though the corpus is not fully explicit on this exact case. E6 says "Declaring its interfaces is what separates it", and E5 says "Declaring the boundary is what makes them two again". E6 also says the separation holds "only where they are declared". Code that "reaches past the interface ... has crossed the boundary" (E6, "Boundary against directory"). O's calls still go to undeclared internals, so O still depends on T across a boundary nobody declared, and E6's blast-radius clause ("any unit that reaches it ... with no declared interface") still applies. The new interface separates T from consumers that use it, but not from O, which remains one system with T for binding and an A3 fault (E5 Boundaries). NOT SETTLED by explicit text: E5's sentence, read alone, could suggest that declaring the boundary is enough by itself. GUESS: the intended reading is that the declared interface has to be the actual crossing, and that is how I read E6 line 17.

### boundary-bypass.B4
The corpus classes each bypass as an A3 fault (A3 Air-Gap Principle; success signal 2, "nothing reaches into another unit's internals"; E5 "The undeclared dependency is itself a fault against A3"). A check that detects such faults is itself one of A3's success signals: "Boundary violations are detectable by review or tooling" (signal 5). For binding, every bypassing component is one system with T, so any change to T's internals answers to all of their axioms (E5, E6 blast radius, A0). A0 says a change that touches a gap closes as much of it as the touch allows. Otherwise the gap is "recorded with its evidence and a revival trigger (M5)", because "larger choices of what work happens next are the director's (R4, AR3)", and "Passing it silently is what the axiom forbids". If the system is multi-agent or autonomous, A10 also asks that the friction become a tracked defect rather than fossilise. The corpus prescribes no specific remediation procedure for a detected set of bypasses, so that part is NOT SETTLED. GUESS: record one entry per bypass with evidence and a trigger, and close the ones your current change touches.

### OBSERVATIONS
- A0 ("where one unit depends on another and no boundary between them is declared") and E5 ("Declaring the boundary is what makes them two again") assume that a boundary is either declared or not. They do not address a declared interface that a consumer ignores. Only E6 (line 17, "only where they are declared", line 44 "reaches past the interface", and the blast-radius row) covers bypass, so a reader of E5 or A0 alone could reach the opposite answer on B1 and B3.
- A0 defines "touched" to include "any consumer whose contract it alters". It does not say whether a change to internals that a bypasser relies on alters that bypasser's "contract in fact" (E5). That leaves open whether closing O's bypass is mandated or only recorded.
- E6 defines adjacency as a shared declared interface, so a bypassing component is not "adjacent" to T. A0's opportunistic-improvement rule therefore does not obviously cover fixing a bypass that lies outside the change's scope, leaving recording as the only explicit path.
- The question premise says the change "does not alter T's behaviour". But for O, which depends on internals, the corpus treats internal behaviour as a de-facto contract. Whether "behaviour" in the premise covers internal function behaviour is ambiguous.
