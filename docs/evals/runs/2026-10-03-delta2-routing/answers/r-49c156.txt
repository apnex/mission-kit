### system-boundary.S1
It answers only to the floor: A3, A4, A8, A9 and A14, the `any-system` row of the applicability matrix (axioms/README.md). The launcher has no traits: it holds no state (T1), has one actor (T3), has an operator watching it (T4 test: "would a human see it before the system acted on the failure?"), and has no model (T5). E5 Boundaries says "A one-shot script with no traits is a system, bound by the axioms every system carries". The service is not altered: A0 says "A system it only composes with, through a contract the change leaves as it was, is not altered, and its axioms do not bind", and E5 gives this exact pair as the example of composing ("A launcher that hands sessions to a service through a declared interface composes with it"). No containing system is declared, so no third system's axioms are added (A0: "every system containing it"). Each of the five binds in the A0 direction sense: the change must not move the launcher away from the limit, and it moves toward it as far as its own scope allows.

### system-boundary.S2
It answers to the launcher's axioms and also to the service's, because A0 says a change answers to "any system whose contract with it the change alters", and E6's blast-radius row says the same for an interface that changes. The union is A1 (stateful); A5, A6, A7, A10, A13 (multi-agent; A7, A10 and A13 also come from autonomous); and the floor A3, A4, A8, A9, A14. A2 does not bind (the service is not declarative), and A11 and A12 do not bind (no model in the loop on either side). One qualifier: if the interface is versioned, E5 says "Publishing a new version of an interface alters no consumer that has not adopted it; each adoption is a change to that consumer". In that case the launcher-side publication answers only to the floor, and the service's axioms bind the service's adoption change. The question does not say whether the interface is versioned, so which of these two readings applies is NOT SETTLED by the facts given.

### system-boundary.S3
It answers to all of the service's axioms: A1, A3, A4, A5, A6, A7, A8, A9, A10, A13, A14. It does not answer to A2, A11 or A12. E5 says "A module inside a service is a system, and the service contains it, whether or not the module's interfaces are declared", and "changing a module changes the service it sits in". A0 says the change answers to "the system it modifies, every system containing it". The module having no state of its own does not matter: E5 "Why precision matters" names this exact case as the conflation to avoid ("a stateful system escapes its own axioms by being described as stateless modules, each of which passes on its own").

### system-boundary.S4
It answers to the library's own axioms only, which is the floor: A3, A4, A8, A9, A14 (the library has no traits; A0 says "a plain library takes `any-system`"). The ten consumers compose with it through a declared, versioned interface that the change leaves as it was, so A0 says they are "not altered, and [their] axioms do not bind". E5 says "each adoption is a change to that consumer, and answers to that consumer's axioms; the component is not altered by being adopted". E5 "Why precision matters" rejects the other reading: "a small shared library would carry the axioms of every system that uses it, and the most-reused code would carry the most obligations". This holds only while every consumer actually uses the interface; E5 says a consumer that reaches past it "stays one system with it", and the question does not say whether any consumer does that.

### system-boundary.S5
With no declared interface, and the service depending on the launcher's output, the two are "one system, with the traits of both" for this purpose (E5 Boundaries; A0 "What brings an axiom into force"). So the change answers to the service's full set as well as the launcher's: A1, A3, A4, A5, A6, A7, A8, A9, A10, A13, A14 (not A2, A11, A12). Something else is wrong: E5 and A0 both say "the undeclared dependency is itself a fault under A3", which breaks the Air-Gap Principle ("units interact only through declared adapters and contracts"). There is also a practical risk: the format change may break the service's parsing, which is A3's Logic Leakage fault. The fix is "Declaring the boundary, and moving the dependency onto it, is what makes them two again" (E5). The change touches that dependency, so by A0 it should close as much of the gap as the touch allows, and record any part it does not close with a revival trigger (RU3). GUESS: the smallest fix in scope is to declare the output lines the service parses as a contract, or to move the service onto the declared session interface, before changing the format.

### system-boundary.S6
Yes, it is defined in entities/E5-system.md: "A system is a unit of engineering with a boundary: what is inside it, and the contracts through which anything outside reaches it." The boundary separates it from a neighbour "only where it is declared and honoured - written down, and the only way the neighbour reaches it". Where that is not true, the two units count as one system. Systems relate in "exactly two ways": contain and compose. E5 also says what a system is not: it is not defined by repository, deployment unit, process, size or traits. E6 adds that a component is "a system seen as a part of a larger system that contains it".

### axiom-direction.Q1
The service is stateful, multi-agent and autonomous, so A1 binds it. A component that keeps important state only in process memory, where other agents cannot see it, is A1's named "Hidden State Problem" ("state lives inside a process; other actors reason about a different reality"). It also works against A5. A0's direction rule shapes your feature: it must not repeat that gap ("new work never repeats a gap the axiom names"), so your feature's state goes somewhere durable that other agents can query. A0 also says a gap in a component your change depends on "must not be built on", so do not make your feature rely on that hidden state. Whether you must fix the component depends on whether your change touches it, meaning it modifies it, newly depends on it, or alters its contract (A0). If it does, you close as much of the gap as the touch allows. If it does not, A0 says "improvement is considered, never mandated": you may close it as a separate change if it is adjacent and passes A14's tangent discipline, and otherwise you record it with evidence and a revival trigger (RU3), because "Either way the gap is recorded. Passing it silently is what the axiom forbids."; which case applies is NOT SETTLED by the facts given.

### axiom-direction.Q2
An axiom is a limit the system is built toward. It is not a pass/fail test. A0 calls it "a load-bearing asymptote", and says "a system is not wrong for being short of it; it is wrong for moving away from it". When you change a system the axiom binds, it gives your change a direction in two parts: "No change may move the system away from the limit, and each change moves it toward the limit as far as the change's own scope allows". In short: never backwards, and forward with every step. The scope is what the change touches; beyond that scope, improvement is optional, but any gap you see must be recorded (A0). A0 names the pass/fail reading as a fault, "The conformance reading", and its CORRECTION banner keeps the old invariant wording on record. A gate is still binary, but what it checks is this direction rule: the change moved the system no further from any limit in force, and repeated no gap it touched.

### OBSERVATIONS
- INDEX.md says links resolve against a local checkout or else github.com, but the task allowed only the local corpus; I read local files only.
- S2 depends on whether the session interface is versioned (E5's adoption rule), and the question does not say.
- S1 and S4: A9 (Chaos-Validated Deployment) binds a one-shot launcher and a stateless library through the `any-system` floor. That reads strangely, but the text is explicit, and A0 itself calls A9 "the pointed case".
- E6 says A14's tangent discipline means the "closer direction" of adjacency. A0 allows opportunistic closing in either direction. These are consistent (E6 says the other direction is "adjacency with less payback, not none"), but you only see that by reading both.
- A1's mandate is absolute ("No functional unit possesses private ... truth"). It is the A0 asymptote reading that stops this from being a pass/fail test, and a reader of A1 alone could miss that.
- I checked one number: A0's claim of "thirty-five relations axioms declare to one another". Counting `related` fields gives 36 entries, one of which is E1, so 35 between axioms, which matches.
